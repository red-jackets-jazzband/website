\title{\textbf{A Glanceable Visual Notation for Jazz Performance Routines}\\[0.4ex]
  \large One round-by-round strip for drummers, rhythm section and front line}
\author{Ben Spoor\\\small Red Jackets Jazzband}
\date{October 2026}

\small
\textbf{Abstract.}
Jazz bands agree their routines in rehearsal: the order of choruses, who solos,
where the band drops out or stops. Existing music encodings represent the order of
sections but not these roles, and corpora of structural annotations record roles only
after the fact. I propose one visual notation of the routine that every member of the
band can read at a glance while performing, including players who may not read the
lead sheet, such as drummers and vocalists. It is a strip of arrows, one per round,
drawn under a tune's chord grid. Drop-outs, solo order and feel (groove and texture)
each get a fixed place and a distinct mark, following research on
preattentive visual features. The notation is a proposal: it has not been tested with
players. I specify a within-subjects study to test it, and outline the coding of
existing routines as further research.

\smallskip
\textbf{Keywords:} jazz, performance routine, head arrangement, music notation,
information visualisation, rehearsal
\end{minipage}
\end{center}
\vspace{1ex}
\end{@twocolumnfalse}
]

\section{Introduction}

A jazz performance has two layers of structure. The \emph{form} belongs to the
composition: a 12-bar blues, a 32-bar AABA song. The \emph{routine} belongs to a
particular band's performance: the intro, the number of choruses, who sings, who
solos and for how long, where the band stops, how the tune ends.
\citet[chs.~11--12]{berliner1994} describes how such arrangements are decided in
rehearsal and filled in through shared conventions. Jazz musicians commonly work from
a lead sheet and a head arrangement, but a head arrangement is by definition not
written down, so there is no documentation standard for the routine.

An unwritten routine depends on every player remembering it, which is weakest with a
new member, a stand-in, or a tune not played for months. A written routine helps only
if it can be read between two phrases, while the eyes are also needed for the music.
Sentences of remarks under a lead sheet are a poor fit, because each must be read
word by word. The routine also matters most to the players least served by the lead
sheet. A drummer has no melody or chords to follow, and a vocalist may sing from the
lyrics without reading the chart, yet both need the routine.

The aim is therefore a \emph{universal} notation: one strip that the drummer, the rest
of the rhythm section and the front line all read, instead of a lead sheet for some
and a private form chart for others. I propose a strip of arrows, one per round,
designed for the songbook of the Red Jackets Jazzband, a traditional-jazz band. The
paper covers the visual form only; how a routine is entered or stored is out of scope.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Encodings.} Conventional notation marks the order of sections with rehearsal
letters, repeats, endings and \emph{D.S.}, coda and \emph{Fine} jumps. Digital
encodings carry the same information: the ABC standard lets a tune header list the
play order of lettered parts with repeat counts \citep{walshaw2011}; MusicXML~4.0
encodes repeats, endings and jumps, and its \texttt{time-only} type restricts an
element to particular passes through a repeat \citep{musicxml2021}; MEI's
\texttt{<expansion>} element lists the performed order of sections
\citep{mei}. All represent \emph{order}. None has a concept of a soloist, an
instrument sitting out, or a change of feel.

\textbf{Annotation corpora.} SALAMI \citep{smith2011} holds over 2,400 structure
annotations of nearly 1,400 recordings, with similarity, function and instrumentation
in separate layers. JAAH \citep{eremenko2018} annotates meter, structure and chords
for 113 jazz tracks. JSD \citep{balke2022} segments 340 jazz recordings into over
3,000 segments, each listing its active instruments and marking the soloist and the
accompaniment. The Weimar Jazz Database \citep{pfleiderer2017} annotates transcribed
solos with chorus and form structure and per-solo metadata, including a rhythm feel.
These corpora show that roles and feel can be annotated systematically, and JSD's
soloist--accompaniment pairing and the per-solo rhythm feel correspond to two of the
marks proposed here. But they are \emph{descriptive}: made after the fact,
time-stamped to a recording, and built for computational analysis. A routine is
\emph{prescriptive} and must be read during performance.

\textbf{Practitioners' form charts.}\label{sec:drumcharts} Drummers have their own,
informal answer: a one-page chart of the song's whole form with only a few bars of
drum notation, outlining the sections with their length in bars (``Verse -- 8'') plus
short cues such as a groove, a hit or an energy level
\citep[e.g.,][]{ellis2025,bergamini2021,ficalora}. Ficalora's Drum Chart Builder app
lets the drummer map song form, grooves and hits \citep{ficalorabuilder}, and the
trade press offers similar guidance \citep{lentz2019}. Beyond drums, the Nashville
number system gives the whole band a compact, section-by-section chart for players who
do not read notation \citep{williams2012}. A blog post by \citet{hein2012} draws songs
as colour-coded blocks along a bar timeline, a visual precedent for the strip's part
boxes that records only section order, length and type. A web template by
\citet{scott} is a contrast: a planning aid for the writer that says nothing about who
plays in performance. Drum charts are personal and count bars; the strip is shared by
the whole band and leaves bar counts to the tune's known form.

\textbf{Horizontal timelines.} Time-based diagrams that place events along a
horizontal axis are well established in other fields. A Gantt chart lays tasks out as
bars against time; its roots go back to Adamiecki's harmonogram of the 1890s and
Gantt's own charts of the 1910s \citep{wilson2003}. WaveDrom draws digital timing
diagrams, one row per signal, from a compact text description \citep{wavedrom}.
Digital audio workstations use the same layout for arrangements. These show that a
horizontal timeline is quickly learned, but they serve planning, hardware design and
production, not a player on stage, and none has marks for a soloist or a drop-out.
The strip borrows the horizontal reading direction and drops the row per signal.

\textbf{Reading at a glance.} Feature-integration theory holds that simple visual
features are registered early and in parallel across the visual field, while
combinations of features require serial, focused attention \citep{treisman1980}.
\citet{healey2012} survey how visualisation design uses such preattentive features to
make a target found without search. For a routine, this argues for answers marked by a
distinct shape in a fixed position rather than embedded in prose. Eye-movement
research on music reading is much less developed than research on reading text
\citep{madell2008}, and its methods vary widely \citep{puurtinen2018}. I am not aware
of any study of how musicians read routine information during performance.

\section{Design}\label{sec:design}

The target task is a player answering \emph{``what do I do this round?''} in one
glance. Six requirements follow: \textbf{R1} each kind of answer has a fixed position
and a mark unlike the others \citep{treisman1980,healey2012}; \textbf{R2} each fact
appears in one position only; \textbf{R3} marks are ones musicians already read (plus
and minus, numbered order, repeat counts); \textbf{R4} a round
with nothing unusual adds no marks, so unusual rounds stand out; \textbf{R5} meaning
is carried by position, typography and signs, not colour, so a printed copy loses
nothing; \textbf{R6} nothing requires reading pitches, chord symbols or
instrument-specific notation.

\subsection{The strip}

The strip runs left to right under the chord grid, one chevron-shaped arrow per round
(Figure~\ref{fig:extended}). Inside each arrow are the round number, one box per part
played (A, B, Intro) and a repeat count (``4x''); an ellipsis box marks a round that
lasts until a cue. Rounds playing the same part share a shade of grey. Under the
arrow come the \emph{lead} (who carries the round), underlined; one
``+\,instrument'' line for each instrument that joins; and any other remarks on a
single line. Three further questions get their own regions: \textbf{who} plays and who
does not (lines under the arrow), \textbf{how} the beat goes (above and below the
arrow), and everything else (the remarks line). ``No rhythm'' answers \emph{who}, so it
is a drop-out, not a feel.

\paragraph{Drop-outs.} A ``$-$\,instrument'' line sits beside the ``+\,instrument''
lines, a matched pair of signs (R3). Strikethrough was rejected because it reads as a
correction. Joins and drop-outs apply to their own round only, so any arrow can be
read without tracing the rounds before it. This mirrors JSD's per-segment pairing of
soloist and accompaniment \citep{balke2022}, expressed as a difference from the full
band.

\paragraph{Solo order.} A solo round shows a numbered sequence under its lead, for
example \textsf{1\,sax/tbn $\rightarrow$ 2\,tpt\,$\times$2 $\rightarrow$ 3\,tpt}. One
solo is one pass through the round's parts, the jazz chorus. A slash marks alternates
(whichever is present), and ``each $\times$1'' fixes the length but not the players.
The lengths sum to the repeat count in the arrow, so the plan can be checked at a
glance.

\paragraph{Feel.} In band usage ``feel'' covers two things I keep apart, each with
its own region and scope (Table~\ref{tab:feel}). A groove usually holds for a whole
tune and changes at most once, so it is drawn only at a change; drawing it on every
arrow would violate R4. Stop time, breaks and similar texture events end with their
round. Half feel and double feel are relative to the groove in force, so they count as
texture. A texture limited to part of a round names that part (``\sc{breaks} in the
2nd B''). Labels come from a short fixed list per axis, which can be translated on the
page; a label outside the list is shown as written. Dynamics (soft, loud, build) are a
third axis that I leave for next steps.

\subsection{One strip for every role}\label{sec:roles}

The strip is universal (R6): every answer is in one of the fixed regions, so no role
needs its own chart (Table~\ref{tab:roles}). No region belongs to one role; the
drummer and the bass player read the same groove label, and the horns and the
vocalist the same lead line. One gap remains for the drummer: the strip gives no bar
counts, which drummers' own charts do.

\section{Proposed evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

The central claim, that the strip is read faster and more accurately than written
remarks, needs an experiment. I specify one here; it has not been run.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
\textbf{H1:} questions about drop-outs, solo order and feel are answered faster with
the strip than with the same information as written remarks. \textbf{H2:} accuracy on
those questions is higher. \textbf{H3:} questions about part, lead and joins are
answered no slower. \textbf{H4:} the gain holds for every role, and is at least as
large for players who do not read the lead sheet as for those who do.

\paragraph{Design and task.} A within-subjects design with two conditions: (a) remarks
as plain text below the music; (b) the strip. Participants are members and stand-ins
of the band, expected $n = 8$--$12$, classed in advance by role and by whether they
read the lead sheet while playing. Routines are counterbalanced across conditions.
Each trial shows a routine briefly (about 2\,s, to be set in a pilot) and asks one
question drawn from Table~\ref{tab:roles}, for example ``In round 5, do you play?'' or
``Where does the beat change?'' Response time and accuracy are analysed with mixed-effects
models with crossed random effects for participants and routines \citep{baayen2008}.
Over a few rehearsals before and after adoption, interruptions caused by questions
about the routine can also be counted. Eye tracking during performance would show where
players look, but its methods vary widely and must be fixed in advance
\citep{puurtinen2018}. With so few participants, only large effects are detectable.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Prescriptive versus descriptive.} The corpora in Section~\ref{sec:related}
describe what a recording did, at the resolution of seconds. A routine states what a
band intends, at the resolution of a round, so the strip shows no times; timing within
a round is a short qualifier (``from the 2nd line'').

\paragraph{Scope.} The distinction between a sticky groove and per-round texture
is the part most likely to transfer to other repertoires. The label lists are
less likely to: they were chosen for a traditional-jazz repertoire and not checked
against other bands' usage. The strip shows the plan that cues steer, not who gives
them; it shows one routine per tune, with alternatives left to remarks; and it gives
no bar counts.

\paragraph{Further research.} The notation should be applied to real routines. Coding
a band's existing routines round by round against the marks would show how often each
is needed, which remarks cannot be classified without the band's input, and whether
the label lists suffice. I have not done this; a second coder would be needed to report
agreement.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The design is untested with players; the readability claim
rests on perception research. The related-work review is selective, not systematic:
``not aware of'' means only that I did not find such a study. That drummers and some
vocalists do not read the lead sheet is an assumption, which H4 tests. Dynamics are not
covered.

\section{Conclusion}

Routine information that standard encodings do not represent can be drawn in a compact
strip: who sits out, as minus lines paired with joins; who solos and for how long, as a
numbered sequence; and how the band plays, split into groove and texture,
each with its own place and scope. Because none of it needs notes or chord symbols, the
same strip can serve every role. The next steps are implementing the strip, running the
study above, adding dynamics, and coding existing routines against it.
